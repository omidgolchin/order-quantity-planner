Why the sales history matters

The right order depends on the whole spread of possible demand, not just the average. The tool fits several forecasts to your history, keeps the one that predicted your own past periods best (a backtest), and uses its errors to estimate how uncertain next period is.

Salvage value

What an unsold unit is worth at the end of the period: clearance price, outlet value, or 0 if it is thrown away. If leftovers keep for next period, use what they are worth then: about the unit cost less what holding them costs. High salvage makes overstocking cheap, so the tool orders more.

Cost of a lost sale

Anything you lose beyond the margin when a customer finds you sold out, such as a customer who does not come back: annoyed customers, penalties. It makes running out more expensive, so the tool orders more. Leave it at 0 if you are unsure.

How the verdict is decided rules, formulas, sources

The order quantity is the newsvendor solution (Arrow, Harris & Marschak 1951): order up to the demand quantile set by your economics, not to the forecast. One unit too many loses (cost − salvage); one unit too few loses (price − cost + goodwill). The balance point is the critical ratio, and the tool orders the smallest quantity whose chance of covering demand reaches it.

critical ratio = (price − cost + goodwill) / (price − salvage + goodwill) order = smallest Q with P(demand ≤ Q) ≥ critical ratio

The forecast is chosen by backtest, not by fit. Seasonal naive, ETS and ARIMA forecast the last 8 periods one step at a time, each time using only the past (rolling origin; Hyndman & Athanasopoulos, Forecasting: Principles and Practice, 3rd ed.). The lowest error wins, and its 80% interval coverage is shown: a method can win on error and still be overconfident. (In your browser, a pasted series races seasonal naive against ETS; the built-in products were also raced against ARIMA offline, by the Python reference implementation in this repository.)

Sold-out periods hide demand. If a period sold exactly the stock on hand, demand was at least that high. With a stock column, those periods are replaced by draws from the sales seen at or above that level before anything is fit (a Kaplan–Meier style fix), which widens the top of the demand range instead of understating it.

Intermittent demand (30% or more of periods at zero) breaks ETS and ARIMA assumptions, so the tool samples past periods directly and says so (Syntetos & Boylan 2005).

Where the numbers behind this tool came from (the 30-product backtest, the classic study this replaces): see Research notes.

How the order size is chosen

This is the newsvendor problem. Order until one more unit is as likely to lose money as to make it. That happens at the critical ratio: underage cost ÷ (underage + overage), where underage = price − cost (+ lost-sale cost) and overage = cost − salvage. The order is the demand level with that probability of not being exceeded.

Chance of running out

The probability that demand next period exceeds the order. Ordering for zero stockouts is almost never profitable; the right level depends on your margins.

Quantile

A level of demand named by how often demand stays at or below it. For example, the 60% quantile is the amount that demand stays at or below in 60% of periods. The tool orders the quantile at your critical ratio (shown in the ORDER LEVEL check), so you run out in the remaining share of periods: exactly the balance your prices make worthwhile.

Profit by order size

For every order size, the average profit over thousands of simulated demand draws from the forecast. The peak is the best order. A flat top means you have room; a steep side shows which mistake (too few or too many) costs more. Drag the marker under the chart to read the profit at any other quantity — it only explores; the recommended order does not change.

How last period is graded what counts as money left on the table

Your realized profit is computed from what actually sold. The plan's order is the recommendation from section 3 for the same product and prices. “Left on the table” compares expected profit (over the whole demand distribution) at the plan's quantity against your quantity: one period is a single draw, so a good order can still lose money and a bad one can get lucky. If you sold out, your sales are a floor on demand, not demand itself: the grade says so instead of treating the period as fully observed.

Gap to the plan

How much more profit the plan's order would have earned on average than your order, given what was known before the period. It grades the decision, not the luck of one period.

Sold-out periods

When you sell out, true demand was at least what you sold, maybe more. Treating that period as exact demand underestimates demand and leads to ordering too little next time. The tool treats sold-out periods as “at least”.
